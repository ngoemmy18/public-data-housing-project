Housing Affordability and Homelessness in Seattle

Seattle's affordability crisis has increased housing burden and contributed to growing housing instability across the city

Image photographed by Mark Angelo Garcia

Why This Matters

Homelessness has become one of Seattle’s most visible and debated public issues. In 2024, King County estimated that more than 16,000 people were experiencing homelessness, reflecting a significant increase in recent years.

At the same time, housing costs across Seattle have risen dramatically. This project explores whether the increasing rent and housing costs burden may help explain broader homelessness trends throughout Seattle.

Data Sources and Methods

This project combines four datasets related to housing affordability and homelessness in Seattle and King County. Rent and apartment vacancy data were collected from the City of Seattle Open Data Portal, while housing cost burden data came from the U.S. Census Bureau American Community Survey (ACS). Homelessness counts were obtained from annual Point-in-Time reports published by the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD).

To prepare the datasets for analysis, unnecessary columns were removed and only relevant years were retained. Neighborhood-level datasets were linked using shared geographic identifiers, while homelessness counts were analyzed separately because they represent county-wide totals rather than individual neighborhoods.

Several limitations should be considered. The homelessness counts are collected once per year and may underrepresent the full population experiencing homelessness. Additionally, homelessness is influenced by many factors beyond rent prices alone, meaning this project identifies relationships in the data rather than direct causation.

Rent and Homelessness Have Increased Together

Over the past decade, Seattle experienced sharp increases in housing costs while homelessness counts across King County also continued torise. Although many factors contribute to homelessness, researchers have argued that housing affordability plays an important structural role.

Between 2012 and 2024, median rent prices and homelessness counts followed similar upward trends, particularly after 2021.

Not Every Neighborhood Was Affected Equally

Rising housing costs were not distributed evenly across Seattle. Some neighborhoods experienced substantially larger rent increases than others, reflecting broader patterns of displacement pressure, redevelopment, and housing inequality throughout the city.

Neighborhoods such as High Point and Rainier Beach experienced some of the largest rent increases between 2014 and 2024.

Spatial Pattern

Housing affordability pressures are not evenly distributed across Seattle. High-burden census tracts appear throughout North Seattle, West Seattle, and South Seattle, while central portions of the city display a mix of both lower- and higher-burden tracts.

Final Takeaways

Seattle’s rising housing costs and growing homelessness trends appear closely connected over the past decade. While homelessness is influenced by many social and economic factors, the data suggests that increasing rent prices and housing cost burden remain important structural pressures affecting housing stability throughout King County. As housing costs continue to rise faster than wages for many residents, more households face financial strain, displacement, and difficulty maintaining stable housing. These pressures can increase vulnerability for lower-income communities and contribute to broader patterns of housing insecurity across the region.

The visualizations also show that housing affordability challenges were not experienced equally across neighborhoods. Some areas faced much larger rent increases than others, reflecting broader patterns of redevelopment, displacement, and unequal housing access throughout Seattle. Neighborhoods experiencing rapid growth and investment often saw dramatic increases in rent prices, making it increasingly difficult for long-term residents to remain in their communities. These uneven impacts highlight how housing affordability is not only an economic issue, but also a social and geographic one that affects communities differently depending on income, development patterns, and access to resources.

Although this project does not suggest that housing costs are the sole cause of homelessness, the data demonstrates how affordability pressures remain deeply connected to housing instability in Seattle. Understanding these patterns is important for policymakers, community organizations, and residents seeking long-term solutions to housing insecurity. By examining rent increases, housing burden, and homelessness trends together, this project helps provide a broader understanding of how structural housing challenges continue to shape daily life across King County.
